\emph{Exploratory: outsourcing dose and unaided performance.} If the deficit in
\S\ref{sec:res-accuracy} runs through outsourcing, participants who outsourced
more should retain less. Three operationalisations point that way; none is
individually decisive (Figure~\ref{fig:outsourcing-dose}). The share of the
eight practice items coded full outsourcing predicted lower post-test accuracy
($-9.5$\,pp over the full dose range, covariate-adjusted, 95\%~CI
$[-24.3, +5.3]$, $p=.21$); among the 61 tool-engaging participants the slope is
$-17.3$\,pp $[-31.3, -3.2]$ ($p=.016$), but only ten
non-fully-outsourcing participants identify it, and leave-one-out $p$ reaches
$.078$. Within participants, where stable person characteristics cancel, each
outsourced practice item in a Bloom category cost $5.5$\,pp on that category's
post-test items ($[-11.6, +0.5]$, $p=.074$). Timing corroborates only weakly:
slower answer adoption predicted higher accuracy within the arm ($+3.3$\,pp per
doubling of $\Delta$, $p=.26$), but the same deliberation-time slope was
strongest in the \emph{search} arm ($p=.001$), so latency indexes post-tool
deliberation generally, not outsourcing specifically. Because outsourcing is a
self-selected behaviour, not an assigned treatment, learners who outsource may
differ in unmeasured effort or motivation; the dose precedes the post-test and
baseline adjustment moves the slope little (dose--Mini-VLAT $r=-.12$), but
causal language is not licensed, and at $n=66$ only correlations
$|\rho|\ge.34$ are reliably detectable. This analysis was not pre-registered
and is reported as exploratory, estimation only.
