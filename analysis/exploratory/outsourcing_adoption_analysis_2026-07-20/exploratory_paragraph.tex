% Answer-level adoption analysis: exploratory paragraph (2026-07-20).
% Not pre-registered (post-commencement); associational register; author sign-off
% required before inclusion. Numbers: analysis.py (seed 2026) / results_log.txt.
% Extraction caveat: single automated coding pass of the assistant's recommended
% option (blind to key and submissions; 100% agreement on a 36-cell blind audit;
% 3.7% of cells below high confidence); recommend a human spot-check before use.

To see what participants actually did with the assistant's answers, we extracted the
option the assistant ultimately recommended in each of the 376 practice-block cells
with interaction and compared it with the participant's submission. Three descriptive
regularities stand out; none was pre-registered, all exposures are self-selected, and
no estimate below is causal. First, the assistant seldom answered in the item's own
answer space: only 19.1\% of engaged cells received a clean option letter, while
76.6\% received a descriptive answer the participant had to translate, and in 65
cells (17.3\%) the recommendation matched no listed option at all --- almost entirely
on the two evaluation items, where the assistant's committed-answer accuracy fell to
0.58 and 0.44 against 0.92--1.00 elsewhere (0.94 overall, $n=295$ committed cells).
Second, participants adopted what they were given: 90.2\% of committed
recommendations were submitted verbatim (95\%~CI $[86.9, 93.5]$), and the 9.8\% of
discordant cells were concentrated where translation was hardest --- adoption was
21.9\,pp lower after a descriptive answer requiring genuine mapping than after a
clean letter (95\%~CI $[-32.7, -11.2]$, $p=.0001$, participant-clustered) --- while
of the 18 incorrect recommendations received, 14 were adopted and only one override
ended in a correct answer. Practice-block abstention shows the same mapping
signature: ``I don't know'' submissions were 8.6\,pp more frequent on
hard-to-map cells (95\%~CI $[+3.2, +14.1]$, $p=.002$). Third, however, none of these
usage measures predicted unaided post-test accuracy over and above the outsourcing
dose itself: adoption rate ($+12.8$\,pp, 95\%~CI $[-28.6, +54.2]$, $p=.54$),
mapping-failure exposure ($-2.3$\,pp, $[-25.9, +21.4]$, $p=.85$), and fast
``blind-copy'' adoption ($-1.3$\,pp, $[-27.3, +24.7]$, $p=.92$) were all null, with
within-person fixed-effects versions equally flat, whereas the dose coefficient
remained at $-14$ to $-17$\,pp in every joint model. The one within-person signal
that survived permutation testing links dose to post-test abstention: categories a
participant outsourced more often drew more post-test ``I don't know'' responses
($+4.5$\,pp per outsourced practice item, 95\%~CI $[+0.4, +8.7]$, permutation
$p=.043$). At $n=66$ these regressions resolve only large effects (full-range
minimum detectable effects of roughly 30--60\,pp), so moderate contributions of
answer-handling style cannot be excluded; what the data do suggest is that the
deficit documented for this arm travelled with how much was outsourced rather than
with how the outsourced answers were handled.
