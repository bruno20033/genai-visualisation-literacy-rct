# "Beyond how much they outsourced, did WHAT they did with the assistant's answers explain the deficit?" (2026-07-20)

Mostly no — and that's informative. When we lined up what the assistant recommended
against what people submitted, the interaction looks even more passive than the
outsourcing counts suggested: nine in ten recommendations were submitted as-is, the
rare disagreements were mostly failed translations (the assistant usually answered
descriptively — "the second scatterplot from the left" — rather than naming a letter,
and on the two chart-critique items its answer often matched no option at all), and
when the assistant was wrong, fourteen of eighteen people copied the error anyway;
only one person overrode a wrong answer and got it right. So there was essentially no
verification happening that could have protected learning. But when we then asked
whether any of this variation — who copied fastest, who faced the hardest-to-map
answers, who adopted most — predicted the post-test, the answer was flat zeros across
every version we tried, while the plain how-much-did-you-outsource dose kept its
usual −14 to −17 point coefficient in the same models. The one wrinkle that survived
our permutation checks: people said "I don't know" at the post-test more in exactly
the topic areas they had outsourced during practice. Read together, and with all the
usual caveats (self-selected behaviour, 66 people, only large effects detectable,
nothing causal): the deficit seems to travel with the outsourcing itself — the
skipped practice — not with the style of copying, and by the post-test the clearest
trace it leaves is people declining to answer where they had let the assistant do
the work.
